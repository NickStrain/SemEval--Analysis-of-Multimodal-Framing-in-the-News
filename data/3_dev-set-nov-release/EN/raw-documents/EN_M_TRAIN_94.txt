Behind EU’s December asylum relocation pledges: More deal-making, fewer transfers

Most commitments made by EU states last December to receive asylum seekers arriving primarily in Italy and Greece are actually alternative forms of support. Also known as relocations, the issue has had a politically toxic history, with the European Commission initially demanding some 30,000 people be distributed annually among member states.

That figure has since been revised down to just over 20,000 (given shorter deadlines) and then further whittled away to under 5,000 and likely even less. An annex in the council’s decision outlined the relocation pledges of some 8,921 people, dispersed among eight member states.

Crucially, it also added a caveat that this could include other forms of support, posing questions on how many are actual relocations. Germany pledged the most with 4,555. In an email to EUobserver, Germany’s interior ministry confirmed not a single one is a relocation.

Germany has instead reached agreements with Greece and Italy to take over the asylum cases of people who first entered those countries before traveling on to Germany. Under the EU’s Dublin rules, initial entry states, such as Greece or Italy, are normally responsible for processing asylum claims.

In practice, most just end up leaving towards northern Europe causing disquiet among national authorities who then impose internal border controls in a failed bit to curtail the movement. Alexander Dobrindt, Germany's interior ministry, described those controls as a "necessary signal" that migration policy has fundamentally changed.

But Germany has also for years been at pains to return people to Italy, which suspended the Dublin agreement in 2022 through a series of emergency measures. A spokesperson from Germany's interior ministry, in an email, confirmed that Berlin had reached agreements with Greece and Italy on its pledges.

She said the agreements mean Berlin's pledge will take "the form of an offset against our burden resulting from the transfer of responsibility for past secondary migration." That means that instead of sending the asylum seeker back to Greece or Italy, Germany will process the claims of 4,555 people already in the country.

This comes despite the EU's own rule book giving priority to relocations, noting that other forms of help are secondary. Germany appears to have been allowed to switch that priority.

Catherine Woollard, a fellow at VUB Brussels University, says this broadly reflects the political compromises necessary to reach an agreement on the EU's first 'annual solidarity pool', which mandates burden-sharing among member states to aid those facing high migratory pressure. "This is not a bad outcome. It is important that the solidarity mechanism is functioning, even if compromises are required," Woollard told EUobserver.

She said more significant issues are also at stake, citing commission efforts to remove unlawful emergency measures imposed by Germany and Italy, which risk up-ending the EU's new asylum rules. But other dynamics over relocations are also at play. The European Commission had in late 2020 proposed so-called "return sponsorships", whereby EU states could deport rejected asylum seekers on behalf of frontline countries, easing migratory strain.

Sponsors got just eight months to execute the return. Failure meant taking the individual into their own territory to wrap up the process. The proposal was ultimately shelved.

While relocations are widely viewed as a positive solidarity measure, some experts caution it can be gamed. And fears are growing that a mutated version of return sponsorships could now creep back in through the relocations.

A country might selectively accept to relocate individuals that they can just as easily deport, says Eleonora Testi, a senior legal officer at the Brussels-based European Council of Refugees and Exiles (Ecre). "It could be that you take everyone from a certain nationality, because you know that they have good relations with that country in terms of returns," she said.

For its part, the European Commission will come out in March with an update on the new asylum rules. The update will provide an overview to what extent EU states have implemented the new rules (which come into force in June) over the past two months. A more thorough annual report is also expected in October.
